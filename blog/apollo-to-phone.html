01 / The comparison

A smaller computer is not automatically a more capable one.

“Apollo had less power than a phone” is directionally true and visually useless. It mixes dimensions, mass, memory, clocking, architecture and workload into a single claim that none of the machines can answer on its own terms.

So this story uses two representations with their own units. The physical scale follows dimensions, volume and mass. The computational scale follows available working memory, clock rate and the number of distinct compute engines. Neither is a benchmark score for the other.

Reading rule. A clock ratio is not a performance ratio. A memory ratio is not a performance ratio. They are concrete capacities that changed together as computers became smaller, denser and more specialised.

What to watch The first drawing retains a common front-face scale; the second retains depth; the final field uses one cell per phone-sized bounding volume. It does not claim that phones pack inside the AGC case.

Front face / same scale

The Apollo Guidance Computer enclosure was about 61 × 32 × 17 cm. An iPhone 17 Pro Max is 16.34 × 7.8 × 0.875 cm. On the same front-face scale, the phone is already about four times shorter and narrower.

Depth / same scale

The depth makes the change clearer. The AGC enclosure is about 17 cm deep. The phone is 8.75 mm deep: roughly 19.4 times thinner by this one dimension. The objects are not merely different shapes; they have different material budgets.

Bounding volume + mass

Multiplying the published dimensions gives an approximate 298× reduction in bounding-box volume. Mass falls from 31.8 kg to 233 g, a 136× reduction. These are enclosure comparisons, not a claim about internal empty space.

03 / Computational scale

The second scale needs more than one ruler.

The AGC was designed for guidance, navigation and control. Its software was shaped around fixed core-rope memory and 2,048 erasable 15-bit data words. A contemporary phone runs an operating system, renders interfaces, processes sensors and media, and schedules different kinds of work across several engines.

There is no defensible single conversion between that workload and an iPhone workload. But there are real, countable shifts in the room available for state, the cadence of the clock, and the kinds of hardware available to execute work.

Working state

The AGC’s erasable memory held 2,048 15-bit data words: 30,720 bits, or 3,840 byte-equivalents. This small, writable state is why its programmers treated memory addresses as a scarce physical resource.

Logarithmic memory scale

A Geekbench Browser sample reports 11.42 GB of system memory for the iPhone 17 Pro Max. Converting both quantities to bytes yields about 2,973,958× the byte-equivalent working-memory capacity. The ratio says nothing by itself about speed; it says how much state can be kept close at hand.

Specialisation

The iPhone does not turn this capacity into one faster version of the AGC. Its A19 Pro combines a 6-core CPU, 6-core GPU and 16-core Neural Engine. Even its reported 4.26 GHz clock is only a 4,160× frequency comparison with the AGC’s 1.024 MHz clock, not a performance verdict.

05 / Keep the units

A compact ledger of the change.

The two scales meet in one object, but they should not collapse into one number. Physical scale tells us how much material, space and carried mass fell away. Computational scale tells us how much state and specialised circuitry moved into what remained.

The takeaway

The change is not that Apollo became a little faster.

It is that computing became physically cheap enough to disappear into an object, while the object gained enough memory and specialised silicon to take on a far broader range of work. The phone is not an Apollo Guidance Computer with a higher score. It is a computer from a different material and architectural era.

Both scales matter. Physical scale explains why the computer can live in a pocket. Computational scale explains why that small object can hold an operating system, a camera pipeline, a graphics processor and machine-learning hardware at the same time.
