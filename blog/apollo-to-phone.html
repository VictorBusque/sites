From the Moon to your pocket.

In 1969 Apollo flew a computer with 2 KB of erasable memory and roughly 85,000 instructions per second. Today a phone in your pocket carries a six-core CPU, a sixteen-core Neural Engine and about 12 GB of working memory. Scroll the curves — frequency, density, memory, parallelism, specialization — that made the leap.

01The baseline

The Apollo Guidance Computer was not a modern CPU with less power. It was a radically different machine optimized to do one job — guidance, navigation and control — in real time, under brutal mass, power and reliability constraints.

Its program lived mostly in fixed core-rope memory; a tiny erasable store held the variables. Software architecture had to become a physical property of the machine, because memory simply did not exist to be wasted.

Block II AGC: 15 data bits + parity · 36,864 fixed-memory words · 2,048 erasable-memory words · 34 normal instructions · 1.024 MHz internal computer clock. Sources at the bottom.
ACT 01 THE BASELINE

A whole spacecraft, concentrated into a tiny memory. The AGC's program lived mostly in fixed core-rope memory; variables lived in a small erasable store. The machine reused addresses aggressively because there simply was not much to work with.

Now the memory budget becomes visible. The fixed store dominates; the erasable store is tiny because variables, counters and state have nowhere else to live. 2,048 words is a hard ceiling, not a soft one.

Every instruction had a price. At roughly 85,000 instructions per second, the machine operated in a completely different performance regime from modern silicon.

And yet it guided a spacecraft. The achievement is not that the AGC was "fast" — it is that software, hardware and mission constraints were designed as one system, tuned by necessity.

"More powerful" begins as a misleading question.

Apollo and the iPhone do not execute the same instruction set, benchmark the same way, or solve the same workload. The useful story is the convergence of several curves at once: frequency, transistor density, memory, parallelism and specialization.

ACT 02 SILICON ACCELERATES

Once the processor became a mass-manufactured integrated circuit, the machine stopped being a spacecraft component and started becoming a platform. The 1971 Intel 4004 put 2,300 transistors on a single chip.

The chip gets denser. The 8086 raised the transistor budget by more than an order of magnitude while becoming the foundation of the IBM PC era — 29,000 transistors.

Now arithmetic becomes a system. The i486 crossed one million transistors and was rated at roughly 15–20 million instructions per second.

The desktop jumps another order of magnitude. By the Pentium III era, 9.5 million transistors could run at 550 MHz in a mainstream consumer processor.

02The desktop era

The Pentium III shipped at 550 MHz with 9.5 million transistors. A few years later, a computer that once needed a desk could fit into a pocket — and still be a general-purpose computer with a browser, a graphics stack and a real operating system.

ACT 03 THE FORM FACTOR CHANGES

Then the computer stopped looking like a computer. The original iPhone arrived in 2007 with a 412 MHz ARM11 processor and 128 MB RAM.

A pocket computer appears. Its memory was already 128 MB — roughly 33,000 times the AGC's ~3.75 KiB of erasable data memory. Same word, "computer"; a different physical object.

And the software stack grows around it. Browser, graphics, sensors, networking, an operating system — the "computer" now lives inside a product people call a phone.

The trajectory is now obvious. The next eighteen years will multiply cores, caches, memory and specialization far faster than the eye can follow.

The remarkable part wasn't the first smartphone. It was what came next.

Once mobile silicon adopted multi-core CPUs, large caches, programmable GPUs and neural accelerators, the "phone" became a heterogeneous computer — different engines, each built for a different kind of work.

Raw speed was no longer the whole game. Specialization is the new multiplier.
ACT 04 HETEROGENEOUS COMPUTE

One chip, many kinds of computers. Modern mobile silicon routes different work to different engines — CPU for general logic, GPU for massively parallel math, Neural Engine for matrix-heavy inference, media blocks for video.

The CPU stops doing everything. Graphics move to the GPU, matrix-heavy inference moves to the Neural Engine, video uses dedicated media blocks. Generality plus specialization.

The A17 Pro, introduced in 2023, reached 19 billion transistors and a Neural Engine rated at up to 35 trillion operations per second.

The A19 Pro pushes the same idea forward — a 6-core CPU, a 6-core GPU with neural accelerators, and a 16-core Neural Engine. The chip is a city of engines, not a single faster core.

03The endpoint

Now put the whole trajectory inside a 233-gram object. The iPhone 17 Pro Max is not simply "a faster Apollo computer." It is a heterogeneous compute platform whose CPU, GPU, neural accelerators, media engines, memory system and thermal design all work together.

The machine is getting smaller. The Apollo computer was physically large, memory-starved and mission-specific; the modern phone carries vastly more compute in the palm of a hand.

The capability is getting denser. Transistors, caches, memory and specialized engines are packed into a fraction of the mass — the rings trace a whole history of capability accumulating.

The architecture becomes heterogeneous. A modern SoC is not one faster CPU; it is a federation of compute engines sharing memory and power.

The computer is now the thing you forget you are carrying. The word "computer" survived; the physical constraints changed beyond recognition.

04The takeaway

We didn't make the computer on the Moon slightly faster — we changed what "a computer" could afford to contain. The Apollo Guidance Computer had to ration memory, arithmetic and power. Modern mobile silicon spends that budget on parallel engines, caches, graphics, machine learning, video, sensors and an operating system, all inside a device that weighs less than a soda can.

What really changed

Transistor density made more logic economically possible. Better process technology made that logic smaller and more energy-efficient. Architecture learned to exploit parallelism. Specialized blocks took work away from the general-purpose CPU. Software grew into the machine instead of being squeezed into it.

What did not change

The fundamental problem is still the same: turn information into actions under constraints. Apollo did it with fixed-point arithmetic, core-rope memory and hand-optimized software. The iPhone does it with billions of transistors, heterogeneous acceleration and an enormous software stack.
