One address, millions of machines.

There are 4,294,967,296 IPv4 addresses — fewer than one for every two people alive — and yet a planet of devices gets online. The escape is a 16-bit field nobody talks about, and a box that rewrites packets and remembers.

01The shortage that made the trick necessary

An IPv4 address is 32 bits, so the entire internet has at most 4,294,967,296 of them. On 3 February 2011, the pool of unallocated addresses at IANA ran empty — the last five /8 blocks went to the five regional registries, and one by one those ran dry too: APNIC in 2011, RIPE NCC in 2012, LACNIC in 2014, ARIN in 2015. There is no barrel left. Addresses now move only by trade and recovery.

Demand never noticed. The world passed 8 billion people in November 2022 — nearly two of us per address before counting a single gadget. A typical US internet household keeps about 17 connected devices (Parks Associates survey, Q3 2023). Count every phone, TV, console and doorbell and the machines outnumber addresses several times over. And yet everything connects.

The escape hatch was proposed in 1994, as a deliberate stopgap: let many machines share one public address, and let a box in the middle rewrite packets so nobody can tell. That box is a NAT — a network address translator — and the field that makes it possible is the port. Thirty years later it carries most residential traffic on Earth, in two sizes: the one on your shelf, and the one your ISP rents by the city.

02An address is 65,536 mailboxes

The port field is 16 bits, so every IP address offers 65,536 numbered slots — and TCP and UDP each get their own full set. Any single conversation is four numbers: your address, a slot you picked, their address, a slot they always use. Your laptop to a website is you:51724 ↔ site:443.

The slots are zoned. The bottom 1,024 (0–1023) are reserved for services that listen on fixed numbers — 443 for HTTPS, 53 for DNS. Clients almost never listen; they grab a free slot from the dynamic range, 49152–65535, the top quarter of the wall, and hold it just for that conversation.

Keep the proportions of this wall in mind. The whole trick of this article is that the wall — not the address — is what a NAT rents out.

03Two fields, then a swap

Every packet carries four numbers that decide its fate: source address, source port, destination address, destination port. NAT's insight is disarmingly simple: if a box in the middle quietly rewrites two of them — and remembers what it did — then any number of private machines can share one public identity.

Private machines use ranges set aside in 1996 for exactly this: 10.x.x.x, 172.16–31.x.x, 192.168.x.x. Your home almost certainly lives at 192.168-dot-something. These addresses are forbidden on the public internet — which is the point: they are infinitely reusable because they mean nothing outside their own network.

Your router is the box in the middle. Watch it work.

Your laptop starts a conversation: a packet FROM 192.168.1.20:51724 TO 93.184.216.34:443. That source address is private — it cannot cross the internet. So the router does something to this packet.

It rewrites the source to 203.0.113.7:50123 — its own public address, and a free slot it just picked — and stamps a row into its translation table. From this moment, the table, not the address, remembers who is talking.

The server replies to 203.0.113.7:50123 — the only address it has ever seen. The router matches the row, swaps the destination back to 192.168.1.20:51724, and delivers. One rewrite out, one reverse rewrite home.

Your phone starts its own conversation from 192.168.1.34:40001. Same public address, next free slot: 50124. Two conversations, two rows, one address — zero confusion.

From outside, your entire household is one machine: address 203.0.113.7, a scatter of numbered slots. That is why every device at home reports the same answer on “what is my IP”.

The rows are rentals. They expire when the conversation goes quiet and vanish on reboot — which is exactly where the trouble starts.

A stranger somewhere sends a packet to 203.0.113.7:50200 — a slot nobody claimed. No row, no match, no delivery: the packet is dropped. The bin's counter is computed from this page's scripted attempt list — three tries, three drops. This is why you cannot host a game server at home just by asking nicely.

Rows also expire. A UDP mapping that goes quiet dies in about 30 seconds; an established TCP mapping can live for days. (Timeouts shown are Linux netfilter defaults — illustrative.) Idle video calls and sleeping multiplayer lobbies disconnect because their rows evaporated, not because the network broke.

The exception is a row you write yourself. Port forwarding is a permanent mapping — slot 25565 always leads to the Minecraft machine, no conversation required. You write it; the router obeys. It is an invitation pinned to the door.

And every row is memory. A pure wire would never need this scene: NAT makes the middle of the network a party to every conversation, tracking each one. Network elders grumble that this breaks the end-to-end principle — the edges were supposed to be smart and the middle dumb. The middle got a ledger instead.

No row, no entry. The table decides everything.

Your ISP runs the same ledger, one size bigger. To it, your whole household — every row of your home table — is a single inside endpoint: 100.64.12.8, an address from 100.64.0.0/10, a private stage reserved in 2012 for exactly this job.

The ISP's router rewrites again: your house's packet leaves as 62.204.7.9:32001 — a public address you share with strangers, wearing a slot that is yours only for now.

Neighbors stack into the same table: one outside address, distinct slots. Rows can number in the millions per device — which is why operators must log every row (protocol, subscriber, address, port, time). When abuse comes from a shared address, that log is the only way home.

Two clerks, no shared notes: that's double NAT. You control the home clerk — you can write its permanent rows. You will never control the ISP's. Its rules, including how many slots you may hold, are the next scene.

04How far does one address stretch?

One public address owns about 64,512 usable slots — 65,536 minus the 1,024 reserved at the bottom; operators may hold back a few more. The entire art of carrier-grade NAT is deciding how many of those slots a subscriber may claim at once. The standard that defines CGNAT (RFC 6888) requires the limit to exist and to be configurable. Turn the dials — every number below is computed from them, in your browser, right now.

At the dial's defaults, a single /24 — 256 addresses, one small pool — fronts over a quarter million devices. Make the ration mobile-tight (256 ports) and the same pool fronts 1.1 million. That is the honest version of the headline: no single address carries a million machines at once, but a pool smaller than a university campus carries them without breaking a rule.

What happens when a subscriber's block is spent and one more app wants out? The standard is blunt: drop the packet, send “host unreachable” if you must, and never evict a neighbor's live row to make room. Port exhaustion at 21:00 on a Sunday looks, from the sofa, exactly like the internet being broken.

05What sharing costs

Address sharing is invisible right up until it isn't. The failures were documented as design issues, not accidents:

DOCUMENTED · RFC 6269

Console gaming

Two players whose households share one outside address may be unable to join each other's lobby — the game sees one player trying to connect to itself.

DOCUMENTED · RFC 6269

Geolocation

Maps and weather place you at the ISP's shared router, not your house. Address-based local results quietly stop being local.

DOCUMENTED · RFC 6269

Login limits

Banks and social networks cap simultaneous sessions per address. Strangers now share your address — and your quota.

DOCUMENTED · RFC 6269

Inbound by design

Peer-to-peer seeding and incoming voice calls need a reachable slot. Behind two uncooperative clerks, that needs a trick — one scene away.

Two homes, two tables, both refusing inbound. First, discovery: each side asks a public broker — a STUN server — “what do I look like from outside?” You learn you are 62.204.7.9:32044. Your friend learns they are 198.51.100.4:61200.

Now the punch: both fire outbound at each other's discovered endpoint, at the same moment. Each router stamps a row before the other's packet can arrive — the hole exists on both sides of the wall.

The rows line up and the conversation runs direct, full speed, no middleman. This choreography — gathering candidates, trying pairs — is standardized as ICE, and it is how multiplayer voice, video calls and WebRTC mostly work behind NAT.

When tables are too strict for the punch, traffic falls back to a TURN relay — a public machine both sides can reach. Slower, always works. Every call you have ever made quietly picked one of these paths.

An address is a lease on sixty-five thousand slots.

06So what is an address now?

Here is the whole answer in plain words. One IP address can front an entire household because every address carries 65,536 numbered ports, and a NAT swaps each machine's private return address for its own address plus a free port, keeping a lookup table so replies can be swapped back. Rent that same trick at ISP scale, ration the slots, and one address fronts an apartment block while a small pool fronts a city — hundreds of thousands to millions of machines that will never hold an address of their own.

The address stopped being a name. It became a lease on slots, and the table — not the address — is what takes your packets home. That is the quiet power shift of NAT: whoever runs the table runs the rules. Your router's table you can edit. Your ISP's you cannot.

The long way out is IPv6: 128-bit addresses, enough to name every device many times over, with no clerk in the middle. About half of Google's traffic already arrives that way (April 2026). Until the rest of the internet finishes moving, the clerk keeps it all moving — one rewritten envelope at a time.

Following packets is this notebook's habit: the journey of one Starlink packet through space is its own note.

Whoever owns the table owns the rules.
