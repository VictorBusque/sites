Index / POST 03 — EUROPE · TECHNOLOGY

A stack ofborrowed defaults.

Europe’s software and cloud dependence is a stack: chips, identity, devices, cloud, apps and data. Follow dependencies, the exits and what sovereignty can mean.

AUG 2026~10 MIN6 SCENESTOPIC: EUROPE

01Dependence has layers

Open a public-service app. It may be written by a local team, run from a data centre in Europe and comply with European law. Yet it can still depend on a foreign mobile operating system, an identity provider, a cloud control plane, a chip architecture, a software update channel and an outside company’s operational knowledge. The app works because all of those promises hold at once.

That is why digital dependence is not one number. It is not the nationality on an invoice, nor the flag outside a data centre. It is the set of components that must keep answering when a service has to continue, change provider, investigate an incident or resist political pressure. The scale is structural: by the European Commission’s own estimate, the EU relies on non-EU countries for over 80% of key digital products, services, infrastructure and intellectual property.

Sovereignty is not a claim that every component must be made locally. It is the ability to understand the dependencies, set the rules and retain a credible choice when one of them fails.

The service at the top is the visible promise: a form, a payment, a medical record, a company’s daily work. It is also the least useful layer for locating dependence; its dependencies are below.

Identity decides who can sign in, reset a password, approve an administrator or issue a token. If this layer is unavailable, a perfectly healthy application may be unreachable to everyone who matters.

Cloud is not just rented machines. Its managed databases, queues, observability tools and deployment controls become part of the application’s design. The more of them it uses, the more specific its exit becomes.

Networks, DNS, certificates and update channels are dependencies too. They decide which name resolves, which code is trusted and which route is available before an application ever gets a request.

At the bottom are hardware, firmware and factories. Europe does not need to own every transistor to make useful choices, but a stack that cannot be inspected or substituted is a stack whose risks are difficult to govern.

02Location is one layer of control

“Our data stays in Europe” answers an important question, particularly for personal and public-sector data. It does not answer every question. A service also has people who operate it, software that updates it, keys that unlock it, a legal entity that receives orders, and suppliers that can interrupt a repair or a release.

None of this makes a foreign provider automatically unsuitable, or a European provider automatically sovereign. It means the label European cloud needs an anatomy. The European Commission’s Cloud Sovereignty Framework, published in October 2025, makes the same move: it scores providers across eight objectives — strategic anchoring, legal and jurisdictional exposure, data and AI, operations, supply chain, technology, security and environmental impact — using 48 criteria and assurance levels that run from SEAL-0 to SEAL-4.

In April 2026 the framework stopped being a paper exercise. The Commission used it to award its own sovereign-cloud tender — up to €180 million over six years, split across four European provider groups, each required to reach at least SEAL-2, the level it calls data sovereignty. When a procurement framework starts grading jurisdiction, the market starts building for the grade.

Start with location. Data stored and processed in an EU region is subject to a particular physical and regulatory environment. That is real, valuable and still incomplete.

Next ask who operates the service. An incident response, privileged support session or infrastructure update can depend on a control plane and staff outside the data room — and on orders from another jurisdiction. When US sanctions targeted the International Criminal Court in 2025, its chief prosecutor reportedly lost access to his Microsoft-hosted email account in The Hague.

Then ask who controls cryptographic access and who can change that arrangement. Customer-held keys reduce one kind of exposure, but they do not make every surrounding dependency disappear.

Control is a chain: place, jurisdiction, operators, keys, code and suppliers. A serious procurement describes the chain and assigns responsibility for each link instead of stopping at the address of a server.

03Openness is an exit, not a logo

Open source is useful because it can make a system inspectable and maintainable by more than one company. Open standards are useful because they make data and protocols legible at a boundary. Neither is a magic spell: an open project can still have scarce expertise, and an open API can still expose only the easy parts. The Commission’s June 2026 Open Source Strategy now treats open source as sovereignty infrastructure — but a strategy is not a maintainer.

The practical question is harder and better: could a capable second team restore the service elsewhere from the documented data, software and procedures? If not, the organisation has a dependency whether or not its software is open.

A contract can promise portability, but a real exit has several different cargos. Data is only one of them. Workloads, identity integrations, operational procedures and the knowledge to run them must travel too.

First make the data legible. The EU Data Act’s cloud-switching rules have applied since September 2025: providers must remove obstacles to switching and support export in machine-readable forms, with charges capped at cost in the meantime. That reduces a barrier; it does not recreate an application.

Then separate what is portable from what is provider-specific. A database schema may move cleanly while a managed queue, identity policy or proprietary observability query needs deliberate replacement work.

The exit becomes credible only when another environment has been tested and people can operate it. From 12 January 2027, the Data Act bans switching charges outright — the Commission has confirmed this includes egress charges for switching. Architecture and practice remain the customer’s work.

A dependency is healthiest when its boundary is visible.

04Build a wider field of choices

The aim is not an isolated European stack. That would be brittle, expensive and at odds with the internet’s useful interdependence. The aim is a market in which European organisations can choose compatible providers, use open interfaces, retain operational knowledge and move a critical service without beginning again from zero.

That asks for more than a new data centre. It asks for procurement that rewards verifiable exits, public investment in interoperable infrastructure, maintainers who can sustain the software underneath it, and institutions that practice recovery. The policy layer is moving: the Commission’s June 2026 Tech Sovereignty Package pairs a second Chips Act with a proposed Cloud and AI Development Act meant to harmonise sovereignty requirements across the single market. The market gap is the harder part — three US providers hold about 70% of the European cloud market, European providers roughly 15% — so law and procurement will be asked to do heavy lifting that only capacity can finish. A cloud, an identity service or a satellite system becomes strategically useful when it gives its users another route.
