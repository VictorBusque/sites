Index / POST 04 — EUROPE · CLOUD

A cloud is morethan a building.

A European cloud needs more than servers on European soil: control of keys, operators, software, supply chain and a credible exit. See the layers that matter.

AUG 2026~9 MIN5 SCENESTOPIC: CLOUD

01The wrong picture is a warehouse

Cloud has a visible half: buildings, racks, fibre and electricity. It also has an invisible half: the software that creates a server, the account that permits an administrator, the keys that protect a database, the support path during an incident and the supplier that ships the next security update. A data centre is the floor of the building, not the building.

This matters to Europe because a local address is often used as a shortcut for control. Location matters. It can improve latency, place processing under a particular legal regime and keep operations near the users they serve. But a service can sit on European soil while its essential decisions are made, deployed or supported elsewhere.

A sovereign cloud is not a marketing adjective for a region. It is a set of capabilities that remain governed, operated and recoverable under the conditions its users actually need.

Begin with the physical site. It holds the machines and keeps data close to users. It is an essential part of a cloud service, but it cannot by itself create, repair or move the service.

Above it sits the control plane: APIs and consoles that create resources, set network policies, grant access and trigger automation. Whoever can operate this layer can shape what the machines below are allowed to do.

Now add jurisdiction, operators and cryptographic keys. Each answers a different question: which laws apply, who can act during a failure, and who can read or alter protected data.

A cloud becomes more sovereign as these links can be governed together and verified independently. The target is not zero foreign dependency; it is fewer unexamined single points of control.

02Portability falls as the service climbs

“Move to another cloud” sounds like one operation, but cloud services live at different heights. Virtual machines and object storage have relatively clear equivalents. The further an application leans into a provider’s managed database, identity, analytics or AI service, the more its behaviour is tied to an implementation that may not have a direct substitute.

That is not a reason to avoid managed services. They can remove real operational work. It is a reason to make the trade intentionally: use them where the gain is worth the exit cost, and record where the architecture has crossed a provider-specific boundary.

Switching begins before a contract ends. A team needs to know what can be exported, which formats are usable, how long the transfer takes and what must continue while it happens.

Data is necessary but insufficient. A working next home also needs infrastructure definitions, integrations, secrets, access policies, monitoring and the operating procedures that connect the pieces.

The EU Data Act addresses part of the power imbalance: providers must remove barriers to switching, support contractually defined transitions and make interfaces available for services beyond infrastructure where appropriate.

From 12 January 2027, the Act removes switching charges, including the charges for data egress needed for a switch. That changes the economics. It does not make a complicated application automatically equivalent somewhere else.

The useful question is not “sovereign or not?” It is “sovereign in which dimension?” Start with data and AI: storage, processing, access and the control of cryptographic material.

Then measure operations. Can EU-based teams operate, support and recover the service without an outside company becoming a mandatory participant? Is the knowledge documented and available?

Supply chain makes the picture wider: hardware provenance, firmware, software packaging, critical subcontractors and the ability to see beyond the first supplier.

Technology completes the view. Well-documented, non-proprietary interfaces and a practical migration route make a service less dependent on any one vendor. The Commission’s framework turns this broad idea into 48 criteria across eight categories.

A cloud earns trust when its exit is believable.

03Build capability, not a wall

Europe can build more useful cloud capacity by treating it as an ecosystem: energy and data centres, yes, but also operating systems, open interfaces, trusted identity, secure hardware, skilled operators and purchasers who demand recoverability. A provider may be European-owned, foreign-owned, open-source-based or a partnership. The meaningful test is what users can verify and retain.

That leaves room for international technology and real competition. It also creates bargaining power: public institutions and businesses can choose a service for its merits without accepting that a single supplier must remain involved forever. A cloud that is more than somebody else’s data centre is one whose users can still make the next decision.
