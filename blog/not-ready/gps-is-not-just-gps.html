01GPS is a clock service with a map attached

We call it GPS when a blue dot moves across a map. The larger idea is positioning, navigation and timing, or PNT. A satellite navigation receiver uses radio signals from orbit to answer three questions: where am I, how am I moving, and what time is it?

GPS is the United States’ system. Galileo is Europe’s civilian-controlled global system. Phones and many professional receivers commonly use several constellations together because more visible satellites make a solution more available and more robust. The trick is wonderfully spare: each satellite broadcasts where it is and what time it sent the signal.

Satellite navigation does not tell a receiver where it is. It gives the receiver several timed radio signals; the receiver calculates its own answer.

One satellite sends a timestamp and its orbital position. By comparing transmitted time with received time, the receiver estimates its distance from that satellite. Its possible position is a huge sphere around it.

A second distance cuts the possible space down to the intersection of two spheres. A third narrows it further. In the real world, each measurement also carries errors from clocks, atmosphere and reflections.

The receiver needs a fourth satellite because its own inexpensive clock is not perfectly synchronised with the atomic clocks in orbit. It has four unknowns: x, y, z and its clock offset.

Four timed ranges let the receiver solve all four together. Location is the by-product of correcting its clock. That is why satellite navigation is a time-distribution system as much as a map service.

Radio waves travel at the speed of light. The receiver measures a delay, multiplies it by that speed, and gets a distance estimate—often called a pseudorange because the receiver clock still needs correction.

That makes timing unforgiving. In one nanosecond, light travels about 30 centimetres. A tiny clock error becomes a visible position error, so satellite systems carry atomic clocks and continuously correct them from the ground.

The message also contains orbital information and clock corrections. A receiver must know where each satellite was when it transmitted, not merely where it appears in the sky when the signal arrives.

Galileo’s ground segment maintains Galileo System Time; GPS maintains its own time scale. Receivers account for the offsets and use the signals together. Interoperability turns several public clocks into a stronger measurement.

02The quiet service is timing

A receiver does not have to be navigating for PNT to matter. Networks coordinate transmission, power systems compare measurements from far-apart equipment, financial systems order events and data centres distribute a common time. The visible blue dot is the friendly interface to a deeper utility.

This is also why a PNT failure can be strange. The failure may not look like a map that points to the wrong street. It can look like equipment disagreeing about sequence, a radio network losing synchronisation, or an alarm whose timestamps can no longer be compared.

The signals arriving from satellites are weak after travelling from orbit. A receiver must separate them from noise, reflections and other radio transmissions before it can calculate anything.

Jamming overwhelms or disrupts the frequencies a receiver needs. It denies the measurement. A receiver may know it has lost service, but timing and position are no longer available from that path.

Spoofing is more deceptive: a transmitter presents a receiver with plausible but false navigation information. The receiver can produce a confident answer that is wrong, unless it has another way to question the signal.

Galileo’s Open Service Navigation Message Authentication, operational since July 2025, lets compatible receivers verify that Galileo navigation data came from the system and was not altered. It helps detect spoofed data; it does not make a weak radio signal immune to jamming.

A resilient receiver does not ask one source to be perfect. It combines compatible GNSS constellations, checks measurements against other sensors and records when the sources disagree.

Inertial sensors can bridge a short loss of satellite navigation by measuring motion from inside the vehicle or device. They drift over time, which makes them a complement rather than a replacement.

Critical timing can also use terrestrial paths and local clocks that hold time when an outside reference disappears. The right mix depends on how accurate the application must be and how long it must continue alone.

Redundancy is not merely two copies of the same signal. It is evidence from different physical paths that can expose an implausible result. The useful question is not “does it have GPS?” but “what happens when GPS disagrees?”

A map pin is only the surface of a planetary clock.

03Why Europe built Galileo

Galileo gives Europe a civilian-controlled global PNT system that is interoperable with GPS and other constellations. That creates better coverage for ordinary receivers, a public capability Europe can govern, and services such as OSNMA that improve the trustworthiness of navigation data. It is not a replacement for every other system; it is a valuable independent contribution to a system of systems.

The same lesson reaches beyond satellites. Infrastructure becomes strategic when daily services quietly depend on it, when its failure has unfamiliar forms, and when alternatives are not ready. GPS is not just GPS because time, location and trust arrive together on the same faint signal from space.
