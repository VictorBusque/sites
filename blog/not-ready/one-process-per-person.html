One process per person.

How WhatsApp carries a hundred billion messages a day: an Erlang process for every connection, queues that live in memory, islands of two nodes that replicate in one direction, and keys the servers never hold. The whole machine, walked end to end.

01The bet on an old runtime

A messenger makes a promise most software never has to make: every message arrives, it arrives fast, and nobody gets disconnected so the company can ship a fix. In a single day in 2014, WhatsApp moved 64 billion messages — a volume rivaling what the entire planet sent by SMS at the time. Lose one, and a person somewhere loses a sentence they meant for someone specific.

WhatsApp was started in 2009 by two ex-Yahoo engineers, Jan Koum and Brian Acton, and its servers were built on a strange-sounding stack: Erlang, a language Ericsson created in the mid-1980s for telephone switches, running on FreeBSD. The first server was a heavily rewritten ejabberd, an open-source chat server that already spoke Erlang. Most of it was eventually replaced, but the language stayed.

The choice was not nostalgia. Erlang was designed for equipment where a crash meant emergency calls failing: its unit of computation is the process, thousands of which live inside one virtual machine, each with private memory, each able to die without dragging a neighbor down. The runtime expects failure and is built to contain it. That is exactly the shape of a messenger's world — flaky phones, dropped sockets, whole countries waking up at midnight on New Year's Eve.

When Facebook agreed to buy WhatsApp in February 2014, the deal was announced at about $19 billion for a company with 450 million monthly users and 32 engineers. Today Meta says WhatsApp serves more than two billion daily users and carries well over a hundred billion messages a day — and the servers still speak Erlang at the core.

WhatsApp bet that a runtime built for telephone exchanges in 1986 was the right tool for a planetary messenger. The rest of its architecture is the discipline of keeping that bet small.

STEP 01 / 05

A phone opens the app and dials one persistent TCP connection to a chat server. No pooling, no multiplexing — one socket per online phone, held open for hours.

STEP 02 / 05

On the server, that socket belongs to exactly one Erlang process. It owns the connection, the session state, and a private mailbox. Nothing about this user is shared with any other process.

STEP 03 / 05

The phone's network dies mid-send. The process crashes — and that is the cleanup. A supervisor restarts it in a known-clean state. One broken session, zero bystanders.

STEP 04 / 05

Now fill the box. WhatsApp's 2012 goal was one million connections per server; a month of fixes got them to two million, and a peak measurement hit 2.8 million on one FreeBSD machine — every one a live process.

STEP 05 / 05

When the phone goes offline, the process simply exits and its memory returns to the VM. The frontier is designed to be cheap to lose — the durable copy of everything lives further in, in queues.

02The path of a message

The servers you just walked through are only the front door. Behind them, WhatsApp runs dozens of separate backend clusters — message queues, presence, groups, media, push notifications, spam filtering, authentication — each cluster one job, each deployed and scaled on its own hardware. A spam-filter crash cannot take delivery down, because they never shared a process to begin with.

Inside a cluster, nodes are fully meshed and pass messages between themselves at the protocol level. Work moves asynchronously by default: a process hands a message to the next hop and moves on, never blocking to wait for an answer. Under a New Year's spike, that asynchrony is the shock absorber — queues grow, then drain, instead of a backlog of blocked callers seizing the machine.

And what does a frontend session do besides hold the socket? On connect it pulls what this user needs — pending messages, notification state, blocking rules — in parallel, close to the edge. Typing indicators and presence updates are batched and rate-limited before they ever reach the core; load stays proportional to activity, not to connection count.

The 64-billion figure is WhatsApp's own record announcement of April 2, 2014: 20 billion sent, 44 billion received, one day. The per-second figure above is computed from it by the code on this page.

STEP 01 / 04

You send a message to someone whose phone is off. It lands in that recipient's personal queue, held in memory in an Erlang term store — one queue per user, keyed by user, nothing shared.

STEP 02 / 04

If it lingers, it is flushed to disk asynchronously, behind the write path, never blocking the send. The message is now safe twice — and the sender was never made to wait for either copy.

STEP 03 / 04

On holidays, disks fall behind while memory doesn't. WhatsApp engineers reported that over 98% of queued messages are delivered from memory before they ever need the disk copy. The spike passes; the queue drains.

STEP 04 / 04

The phone reconnects — to any node — and the queue follows the user: deliver, wait for the recipient's acknowledgment, then delete. Not everything gets this guarantee: typing indicators and receipts ride the same wires with no delivery promise. Messages are certain; "typing…" is not.

A message is a promise. Everything else is containment.

03Storage, shrunk to keys

There are no joins in the hot path, and no cross-table transactions. WhatsApp's server state looks like a key-value store: every user, session, and message pointer is a key with a compact value, held in Erlang's in-memory term stores, with Mnesia tables underneath for what must survive a restart.

Writes go through in async_dirty mode — accepted immediately, without transaction ceremony — and land on disk through separate transaction managers, spread across directories and devices so a slow disk cannot serialize the system.

The trick that makes it scale: every table is cut into fragments, and each fragment is owned by a single process on a single node. One owner means serialized access with no locks. More fragments, more owners, more throughput — without reshuffling anyone else.

STEP 01 / 05

Take the fragments from the last section and give each exactly one home: an island — a small group of nodes that owns a slice of the keyspace and nothing else.

STEP 02 / 05

Which island holds which user? A hash decides, deterministically, with no lookup service and no master to ask. Every node can compute the answer alone — including a client reconnecting after a failure.

STEP 03 / 05

Inside an island, one node is primary and owns the writes; a secondary receives a one-way, asynchronous replication stream. No two-phase commit, no distributed locks, no bidirectional sync to get wrong.

STEP 04 / 05

The primary dies. The secondary promotes and becomes the new owner; replication flips to point at it. Clients reconnect to the promoted node and resume. Failover is scoped to one island, and takes seconds.

STEP 05 / 05

This is the whole resilience story in one picture: one-way replication because coordination is what kills distributed systems, and islands because the smallest possible blast radius is two nodes — never the fleet.

04Deploy without goodbye

The last piece of the availability puzzle is the most Erlang-flavored one. A fleet of restart-based servers can only ship a new version by disconnecting everyone, politely, one box at a time. Erlang was built for switches that could not hang up on a caller, so the runtime can load a new version of a module into a live virtual machine: running processes finish their loop and pick up the new code on the next call, sockets and queues untouched.

MODE A · THE CLASSIC

Restart the fleet.

Drain a server, disconnect its sessions, restart the process, let phones reconnect and repeat the storm across the fleet. Safe if you are careful, slow even when you are, and every deploy is a small outage rehearsed at scale.

MODE B · WHATSAPP

Swap the code, keep the socket.

Load the new module into the running VM. Processes migrate to it on their next message loop; state lives in shapes both versions understand. Upgrades roll across the fleet with zero disconnects — engineers shipped routinely without dropping a single connection.

05When it broke

None of this made WhatsApp unbreakable; it made breakage survivable. Two incidents from the engineering talks became legend. In one, two subsystems used the same hash function with the same seed, so their keys collided and thousands of entries piled into single buckets. Changing the seed — a two-line patch — improved that subsystem's throughput by roughly 4×.

The other started in a router. A VLAN was silently dropped, a cluster's connections collapsed, and millions of phones began reconnecting at once — a thundering herd. Internal queues jumped from empty to four million messages; even Erlang's normally robust process groups started behaving badly. The recovery was deliberate: shut it down, bring nodes back one by one, let the mesh re-form. The lesson stuck — WhatsApp treats failure as an input to the design, not a surprise.

Isolation did not prevent the storm. It decided whose problem the storm was.

STEP 01 / 05

April 2016: WhatsApp turned on end-to-end encryption for every chat, using the Signal Protocol. Keys are generated on your phone at install — a long-term identity key, a signed pre-key, a stack of one-time keys. The server stores the public halves, and only those.

STEP 02 / 05

To open a session, your phone fetches the recipient's public keys and mixes them with a fresh ephemeral key through three or four ECDH handshakes. Both phones derive the same root key. The server watched the exchange happen and learned nothing it can use.

STEP 03 / 05

Every message gets its own key — 80 bytes: an AES-256 key, an HMAC key, an IV. Keys ratchet forward per message and per round trip, and used keys are destroyed. Steal today's key and you read nothing tomorrow; steal the phone and yesterday stays sealed.

STEP 04 / 05

The server's job shrinks to routing opaque blobs. Groups use Sender Keys: one ciphertext, and the server fans it out to every member. A photo is encrypted on your phone and stored as a blob; what travels in the message is a pointer and the keys.

STEP 05 / 05

2021, multi-device: your laptop links without your phone online, because every device now has its own identity — and your phone encrypts separately to each device of each recipient. The account is a mapping; the trust lives on the devices.

06Why it worked

Strip the details and WhatsApp's architecture is four walls of containment. Processes contain crashes: one user's broken session dies alone. Islands contain hardware: one failed box is two nodes' problem. One-way replication contains coordination: there is no distributed transaction to wedge under load. And end-to-end encryption contains the operator itself — even a compromised server learns nothing it can read.

The deeper lesson is about team size. Thirty-two engineers ran a planet-scale service because the system fit in their heads: one language, one VM, small clusters, one job per cluster, every line of early backend code reviewed by the founders. Simplicity was not an aesthetic — it was carrying capacity. Every abstraction they skipped was an outage they never had.

The stack was old and the discipline was modern. That combination — a runtime built in 1986 for equipment that could not fail visibly, operated like a product that could not fail at all — is what let a team smaller than most startups' platform groups carry a significant fraction of human conversation.

Scale was never the trick. Containment was.
